10. Walls and signal loss

Contents

A wall eats some of the Bluetooth signal on its way from a phone or tag to a receiver. Walls you draw on the map tell PadSpan how much. That is what keeps a phone in the kitchen from being counted in the room behind the kitchen wall, and the neighbours' phones out of your living room.

You don't need every wall. Draw the ones between rooms that get mixed up, and the outside walls if neighbours' phones show up inside. Walls need a map with a scale (chapter 9), because they are stored in metres.

Drawing a wall

  1. Open Mapping → Edit and pick the Signal walls tool.
  2. Pick the Material (below). Name the next wall if you like.
  3. Click on the map to start the wall, click at each corner, and finish it. It is saved the moment you finish. Undo point takes back the last click; Cancel drawing drops it.

Every wall you draw gets the material that is picked, until you change it. The list starts on Metal, the strongest, so pick the material first.

Enclose (3dB walls) draws thin 3 dB walls round the picked room in one go: good for ordinary interior walls.

The materials

Each material is one number, in dB: more dB, more signal lost going through it.

MaterialLossWhat it's for
Open (Loft) — no wall0 dBNo wall: an opening to the floor above or below, or a room edge with no wall on it.
Brick4 dBA single brick or block wall.
Custom6 dB, or what you setAnything else. A wall with no material counts as Custom 6 dB.
Concrete8 dBPoured concrete, foundation walls.
Metal12 dBSteel doors, metal studs, foil-backed insulation, plaster on wire mesh.

Pick Custom and a Loss through it box appears (0 to 30 dB). Set it before you draw.

These are round starting numbers, not measurements of your house. They are on the gentle side on purpose: Bluetooth gets round a lot of walls through doorways, gaps and ceilings, so the loss PadSpan sees is usually less than the wall alone would cost.

Floors are different. Each floor between two places starts at 10 dB, and PadSpan learns the real figure for each pair of floors from your own data once it has enough. Walls aren't learned: it is the number you pick.

Is my house anywhere near that?

Figures often quoted for Wi-Fi planning at 2.4 GHz, the band Bluetooth uses, give a feel for it. They vary a lot with thickness, damp and what's inside the wall.

Real wallRoughly
Drywall or plasterboard on wood studs2–4 dB
Wooden door, ordinary window2–4 dB
Brick, single course6–10 dB
Concrete block6–12 dB
Poured concrete, stone10–20 dB or more
Old plaster on wire (metal) lath10–20 dB, sometimes much more
Low-E or coated window glass10–30 dB
Metal: steel doors, foil insulation, a fridge, ductwork15–30 dB or more

Old houses catch people out twice:

  • Plaster on lath. Wooden lath does little. Expanded metal lath (common from the 1920s on, and in repairs) acts like a screen. If a wall like that sits between two rooms that keep swapping devices, draw it as Metal, or Custom at 12 to 20 dB.
  • Thick masonry. A solid stone or double-brick wall is nearer Concrete or Custom 10 to 15 than Brick.

Open edges: rooms with no wall between them

A loft, or a kitchen and living room that are one open space: signal already passes where no wall is drawn, but marking the edge open records it on the map, and the 3D house leaves the wall out there.

  • Open edge: tap a room edge: tap a room's edge on the map. Only the stretch it shares with the room next door opens; an edge with no neighbour opens whole.
  • Open all edges gives every edge of the picked room an Open (0 dB) wall. It asks first.
  • Remove open edges takes back the open edges this tool marked along the room.

A room's edges can be marked once its outline is saved. In Live Aboard, an open edge can have a glass railing or none: the Railing button on its row (chapter 16).

Doors and windows that open

A steel door blocks signal when it is shut and none when it is open. PadSpan can follow that with the door's own sensor.

  1. On a wall's row, choose Door (Mark a door/window on this wall).
  2. Click the two ends of the door on the wall. Pick again starts over.
  3. Choose its Linked sensor, a Home Assistant door or window sensor, and its Door/window material (it starts as the wall's own).
  4. Choose Create door/window.

A Metal door or window loses its 12 dB while its sensor says open, and gets it back when shut. PadSpan waits for two readings in a row before it changes, so a flickering sensor can't make rooms jump. A door of any other material is shown open or shut on the maps and changes nothing for the signal: ordinary doors and glass hardly block Bluetooth either way.

A receiver that hears the neighbours

Walls help only when they are between the phone and the receiver. A receiver on an outside wall, or on a floor above a shop or a flat, also hears the street and the people downstairs. For that one receiver, Bluetooth → Scanners → Min signal (Min signal: off unless you set it) ignores anything it hears weaker than the level you pick, say Ignore < -90 on an outside wall or Ignore < -80 by a front door.

  • Positioning and Occupancy's phone count stop using those faint readings from that receiver.
  • Everything else still sees them: the receiver and advert lists, Forensics, Traceback and calibration.
  • A phone in the room reads far stronger than that at its nearest receiver, so it isn't lost.

How to tell a wall is wrong

  • A phone or tag sticks to the room on the other side of a wall: the wall is weaker on the map than in real life. Go one step up (Brick, then Concrete, then Metal), or Custom with a bigger number.
  • A device in a room can't seem to hear the receiver right beside it: there may be a wall drawn between them that shouldn't be there, or one much too strong.
  • Calibration helps either way. It fits how fast signal fades in your house, which soaks up a lot of what the walls don't get right: chapter 11.
